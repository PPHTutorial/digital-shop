The Four-Day Work Week Experiment, One Year Later

0

A year into a genuinely reduced four day work week, rather than the compressed hours version some companies quietly substitute for it, the results at companies that tried it honestly are more interesting and more mixed than the early enthusiastic coverage suggested.

Productivity per hour genuinely rose, but not enough to offset every role

Across companies that tracked this carefully, output per hour worked did increase meaningfully for roles involving focused individual work, writing, design, development, likely because a shorter week forces sharper prioritization and reduces the low value busywork that expands to fill a longer week. Roles requiring constant availability, customer support, certain operational functions, saw a much weaker version of this effect, since those roles are bounded by external demand rather than internal focus and priority setting.

Meetings shrank more than actual work did

The most visible early change at most companies trying this was a sharp reduction in meeting time, both in frequency and length, as teams adapted to a genuinely constrained schedule. Many participants reported this alone accounted for a large share of the time savings that made a four day week feel achievable at all, which raises an uncomfortable question about how much meeting time in a standard five day week was ever truly necessary.

Where it struggled

  • Client facing roles where customers expect five day availability found the transition genuinely difficult without adding complexity around coverage and scheduling.
  • Smaller teams with less redundancy felt staffing gaps more acutely than larger teams that could rotate coverage more easily.
  • Some employees reported the same total workload compressed into fewer days, effectively creating longer, more intense days rather than a genuine net reduction in hours.

The freelance and solo creator version looks different

For self-employed people without a company-wide policy to opt into, informally moving toward a four day working pattern showed similar productivity patterns, sharper prioritization, less unproductive drift, but came with the added complexity of managing client expectations around availability, since clients accustomed to five day responsiveness do not automatically adjust their expectations just because a freelancer decided to.

What made the difference between success and failure

Companies and individuals that treated the fifth day off as an opportunity to genuinely eliminate low value work, rather than simply compress the same workload into fewer days, reported far higher satisfaction and sustainability a year in. The change that mattered was not the calendar shift itself but the harder, ongoing discipline of continuously questioning which tasks and meetings actually justified the time they consumed.

Is it worth trying

A year of real data across multiple organizations suggests a genuine four day week is not a universal win, but it is also not the gimmick some skeptics assumed. It works best for roles built around focused, individual output and worst for roles bound by constant external demand, and its success depends far more on a genuine willingness to cut low value work than on the schedule change alone. Anyone considering it, whether as a company policy or a personal freelance experiment, should expect the actual challenge to be prioritization discipline, not simply fitting five days of work into four.

Trust between managers and employees turned out to be the real variable

Teams where managers already trusted employees to manage their own time well before the schedule change adapted to a four day week with relatively little friction, while teams with a pre-existing culture of close oversight and skepticism about employee autonomy struggled considerably more, often regardless of the specific role or industry involved. This suggests the schedule change itself was less predictive of outcome than the underlying management culture it was introduced into, a factor rarely mentioned in the initial enthusiastic coverage of early pilot programs.

What a year of data suggests about long term sustainability

Companies that treated the first few months as an experiment with a genuine willingness to revert if it was not working reported more honest, useful feedback from employees than companies that had already publicly committed to making it permanent before gathering real data, since employees in the latter group sometimes hesitated to raise genuine concerns about a change already treated as settled. Building in an explicit, stated review point before declaring any schedule change permanent produced more honest internal feedback and, ultimately, better long term decisions about whether the change was actually working.
